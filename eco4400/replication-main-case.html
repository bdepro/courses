The Setting

A law firm has hired 4400ECO Advisors to check whether Cheng & Hoekstra's finding on castle-doctrine laws is solid enough to rely on, before it shows up in the firm's own work. Your job on this engagement: replicate the number, test it against a method built specifically for a case like this one, and say plainly whether the effect is big enough to matter. Statistical significance alone won't answer that question.

Twenty-one states adopted castle-doctrine laws between 2005 and 2009, in five different years. The warm-up sidestepped that entirely: it kept only the 2006 cohort, the largest one, and compared 2005 to 2006. That restriction is exactly what let the arithmetic stay simple enough to do by hand five different ways, and it left out the other 15 states entirely.

This stage puts the staggered timing back in. You already have the tool for it: castle.dta, the same file from the warm-up. Nothing new to download or learn about the data. What changes is the question: does a standard regression still work once treatment doesn't start on the same date for everyone, and if not, what does?

The Task: Three Parts

1
The Bridge
Run the warm-up's Method 4 (twoway fixed effects) again, this time on the full panel, covering every cohort across the 2000-2010 window. castle.dta already has cdl, Cheng & Hoekstra's own treatment variable, built for exactly this. Your number should match their published Table 5, Column 1 almost exactly — and it will still be wrong, in a way the next part explains.
2
The Lee & Wooldridge Fix
Collapse each state's history to a single number instead of pooling everyone into one regression. Treated states use their own adoption year as the pre/post split. Control states have no adoption year of their own, so they blend every cohort's split instead, weighted by cohort size (equations 7.11–7.12 and 7.18). Once every state is one number, an ordinary cross-sectional regression gives you tau_omega — their fix for exactly the staggered-timing problem Part 1 ran into.
3
Eyeball Comparison
Line up your Bridge estimate, your tau_omega, the warm-up's 0.108, and Cheng & Hoekstra's own published range. None should match exactly — they're different specifications on different slices of the same idea. What matters is whether they agree on sign and land in the same neighborhood.
Why doesn't the Bridge fully fix it? A standard twoway-fixed-effects regression on staggered-adoption data can implicitly use already-treated states as part of the comparison group for later-treated states, inside some of its underlying 2x2 comparisons. Goodman-Bacon (2021) calls these "forbidden comparisons," and shows they can bias the pooled coefficient even though nothing about the regression looks wrong on its face. castle.dta is one of the standard textbook examples for this exact problem — which is why Lee & Wooldridge needed a different approach.

A Note on Two Different "Weights"

It's easy to conflate these, since they're unrelated ideas that happen to share a name. Cohort weights are Lee & Wooldridge's actual method in Part 2: how to blend a control state's comparison across every possible treatment-year split. Population weights show up only in Part 1's Bridge regression and in Cheng & Hoekstra's own published tables — a standard choice to give larger states more influence, with nothing to do with staggered timing. Lee & Wooldridge's method does not use population weights at all.
