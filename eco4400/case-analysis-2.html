Key Terms

  • Fixed cost: a cost that stays the same whether AIG writes one CDS contract or one thousand. Think of costs like office buildings, executive salaries, or technology systems that don’t change just because the company writes more business.
  • Variable cost: a cost that changes as output changes. The more CDS contracts AIG writes, the more costs it takes on to support, monitor, and manage those contracts.
  • Opportunity cost: every dollar spent on one opportunity is a dollar that can’t be spent somewhere else. That’s why economists care so much about opportunity cost. If AIG ties up billions of dollars supporting its CDS business, what else could that money have been used for? Paying down debt? Investing in another business? Returning money to shareholders?
  • Accounting cost & accounting profit: the costs and profits reported on the financial statements. Accounting profit equals revenue minus the expenses the company records. It does not include the value of missed opportunities.
  • Economic cost & economic profit: this takes accounting one step further. Economic profit asks, “Was this the best use of the company’s money?” It subtracts the opportunity cost of capital from accounting profit. A company can report healthy accounting profits and still make poor economic decisions if shareholders could have earned a better return somewhere else.
  • Hidden-cost fallacy: forgetting to include a cost that really should matter when making a decision. You didn’t count all the costs.
  • Sunk-cost (or fixed-cost) fallacy: letting money that’s already been spent influence your decision, even though you can’t get it back. The question isn’t, “What have we already spent?” It’s, “What’s the best decision going forward?”
  • Finite reinsurance: a special type of reinsurance that transfers very little real risk. Regulators believed AIG used one of these deals to make its financial statements look stronger than they really were instead of actually reducing risk.

Chapter 3: The Decision Framework

As consultants, one of your most important jobs is figuring out which costs actually matter. Some costs change depending on the decision your client makes next. Others don’t. Chapter 3 is about learning to tell the difference and recognizing that a business can look profitable on its financial statements while creating far less value than it appears.

A fixed cost doesn’t change because of the decision you’re making today. A variable cost does. Opportunity cost is different. It’s the value of the next-best alternative you give up when you choose one option over another.

Here’s where economists start thinking differently. A business can look profitable on its financial statements and still be making a poor economic decision. Accounting profit is simply revenue minus the expenses a company records. Economic profit asks one more question: what else could that money have earned? It subtracts the opportunity cost of the capital tied up in the decision. If that capital could have earned more somewhere else, the business may look successful on paper while actually creating less value than it appears.

Two fallacies to watch for:

  • The hidden-cost fallacy is forgetting to count a real cost that actually matters to your decision.
  • The sunk-cost (or fixed-cost) fallacy is letting money you’ve already spent, and can’t get back, influence a decision about what to do next.

    Here’s a simple example that has nothing to do with AIG. Imagine you bought a $50 concert ticket, non-refundable, and on the day of the show you’re feeling sick and would honestly rather stay home. Do you go anyway because you already paid for the ticket? Chapter 3 says no — the $50 is gone either way. The only question that matters now is whether going to the concert tonight is worth more to you than staying home. That’s exactly the kind of thinking this case is asking you to practice, except the dollar amounts are AIG’s, not yours.

Client Situation

Martin Sullivan has been CEO for only three weeks. The accounting scandal began before he took over, but every decision from this point forward belongs to him. One of the biggest is whether AIG should continue expanding its credit default swap business, a strategy the Board approved just a few months earlier.

This isn’t an easy decision to reverse. AIG has already invested heavily in the CDS business by hiring employees, building systems, developing relationships with counterparties, and compiling years of data showing very few losses. On paper, it has been one of the company’s most profitable businesses. Walking away now would mean slowing a business that has been generating strong profits and appears to be working exactly as intended.

But the world has changed. AIG is no longer the company it was in January. The accounting investigation, leadership changes, financial restatement, and credit rating downgrade have all changed the environment in which Sullivan must make this decision. The question isn’t whether the CDS business caused the accounting scandal. It didn’t. The question is whether these new events change the risks of continuing to expand it.

As consultants, your job isn’t to decide whether expanding the CDS business was the right decision in January. Your job is to decide whether it is still the right decision today. Should Sullivan continue expanding, pause the strategy, or change course? Base your recommendation only on the information available on April 4, 2005.

Economic Backdrop — Spring 2005

Feb. 2005
The Investigation
New York Attorney General Eliot Spitzer and the SEC began investigating AIG over a $500 million transaction with General Re, a Berkshire Hathaway company. Regulators alleged the deal made AIG’s financial statements look stronger than they really were without meaningfully changing the company’s underlying risk — AIG recorded the transaction as if it had transferred insurance risk, when in reality AIG still bore much of that risk. The investigation quickly became a major blow to the company’s reputation.
Mar. 14, 2005
Greenberg’s Resignation
AIG announced that longtime CEO Maurice “Hank” Greenberg was stepping down after nearly 38 years. Martin Sullivan became the new CEO, and the company named a new CFO while the accounting investigation continued. For investors, employees, and customers, this was a clear sign the problems at AIG were becoming more serious.
Mar. 2005
The Financial Restatement
AIG’s 2004 annual report revealed the company had overstated its earnings by nearly $4 billion over the previous five years and reduced shareholders’ equity by $2.26 billion. For investors, this raised a much bigger question: can we still trust the numbers? Standard & Poor’s placed AIG’s AAA credit rating under review, raising concerns that a downgrade could follow.
Mar. 2005
The First Real Cost of a Downgrade
Fitch lowered AIG’s credit rating from AAA to AA because of the accounting investigation and leadership changes. Some of AIG Financial Products’ CDS contracts required the company to post additional collateral if its own credit rating fell — that single downgrade immediately required AIG to post $1.16 billion in cash. That money wasn’t lost, and it wasn’t an accounting loss, but it was no longer available to lend, invest, or grow the business. The downgrade created a very real economic cost long before homeowners stopped making mortgage payments.
Apr. 3, 2005
The New CEO’s Message
New CEO Martin Sullivan wrote to shareholders, promising AIG would fully cooperate with regulators. At the same time, he emphasized that he believed AIG’s core business remained strong and that the company would continue moving forward under its new leadership.
This is an important lesson in economics. Some costs don’t show up because a business is losing money. They show up because the business suddenly has fewer options. One downgrade tied up more than $1 billion that could no longer be used elsewhere, making the CDS business more expensive to operate even before large insurance claims began.

Note: this figure comes from the Darden/HBP case “American International Group, Inc.—The Financial Crisis” (your Chapter 1 required reading), which reports it citing Michael Lewis’s account in Vanity Fair, “The Man Who Crashed the World” (2009).

Digging deeper: is AIG measuring the cost correctly?

On paper, AIG Financial Products’ CDS business looks extremely profitable. The company has collected far more in premiums than it has paid in actual losses. But Chapter 3 pushes us to ask a better question. Is AIG counting all of the costs? The business looks profitable. But is it actually creating value?

Every new CDS contract ties up capital and creates the possibility that AIG may need a large amount of cash quickly. That capital cannot be used somewhere else, which means it has an opportunity cost. The business may show a strong accounting profit while looking much less attractive once we include the true cost of the capital, liquidity, and risk it requires.

So the question is not simply, “Is this business making money?” The better question is, “Is this still the best use of AIG’s resources?”

Remember: a business can be profitable on paper and still be a poor use of capital. That’s exactly what you’re trying to determine.

A note on missing information.

You will not have access to AIG Financial Products’ internal cost records or an exact measure of how much capital the CDS business is tying up. That information was not public on April 4, 2005. That does not mean you can ignore it.

State one specific and reasonable assumption about the amount of capital or collateral the business requires compared with the accounting profit it generates. Explain why your assumption makes sense based on the information in the case, then use it consistently throughout your analysis.

Real consultants almost never have perfect information. Neither will you. What matters is making thoughtful assumptions and using them consistently. The goal is to show that you understand how missing information affects a decision and how a consultant can still build a thoughtful recommendation without pretending to know more than the client knows.

What we know

  • January 2005: AIG’s Board approved continued expansion of the CDS business. The business had been highly profitable on paper because claims had remained very low.
  • March 14, 2005: CEO Maurice “Hank” Greenberg resigned (forced out). Martin Sullivan immediately became AIG’s new CEO.
  • Financial restatement: AIG restated its financial results, reducing shareholders’ equity by $2.26 billion. S&P placed AIG’s AAA rating on negative watch.
  • A real cost: after Fitch downgraded AIG from AAA to AA, the company was required to post $1.16 billion in collateral under some of its CDS contracts — cash AIG could no longer use elsewhere.
  • Keep the issues separate: the accounting investigation focused on AIG’s reinsurance accounting, not its CDS business. But it changed AIG’s financial position, reputation, and credit rating, all of which affect today’s decision.

What we don’t know (and must assume)

  • How much capital is really tied up in the CDS business? We don’t know the true opportunity cost of that capital or what return AIG could earn by using it elsewhere.
  • Will AIG’s financing costs increase? We don’t know whether the credit watch and recent downgrade will lead to higher collateral requirements or make it more expensive to do business in the future.
  • Can AIG use these resources somewhere else? We don’t know whether the people, systems, and relationships built for the CDS business could be successfully redirected elsewhere or whether those investments are effectively sunk costs.
The question before the Board: Looking only at the information available on April 4, 2005, should AIG continue expanding its credit default swap business?

As you make your recommendation, separate sunk costs from relevant costs, consider the opportunity cost of AIG’s capital, and decide whether the CDS business is still creating economic value or simply showing strong accounting profits.
