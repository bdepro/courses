Key Terms

  • The Rational Actor Paradigm: the assumption that people generally make the best decision they can based on the information they have and the incentives they face. Under this lens, a bad decision usually traces back to a missing piece of information, a misaligned incentive, or both.
  • Incentives: the book breaks this into two pieces — a metric (some way of measuring how well someone is performing) and a link from pay to that metric (a compensation scheme that actually rewards good performance or punishes bad performance). A decision-maker can have all the information in the world and still make a poor call if either piece is missing.
  • The Diagnose/Improve framework: the two-step process this course reuses all semester. Diagnose: who made the decision, what information did they have, what incentives did they face? Improve: reassign the decision, give better information, or change the incentives.
  • Credit default swap (CDS): a contract where one party pays a regular fee (called a premium) and the other agrees to pay if a specific bond or loan defaults. Think of it like insurance on debt, except the buyer doesn’t have to own the debt to buy protection.
  • Mortgage-backed security (MBS): a bond created by bundling thousands of individual home mortgages together. Homeowners make monthly mortgage payments, and those payments flow to investors who own the bond.
  • Mark-to-market: valuing an asset or contract at its current market value instead of its original value. If the market value of an insured security falls, a company may have to post cash immediately, even if no one has actually defaulted.
  • Collateral posting: cash or securities a company must provide as a good-faith guarantee when a contract moves against it, often before any actual loss occurs.
  • Liquidity risk: the risk of not having enough cash available to meet obligations today, even if the company appears financially healthy over the long run.
  • Marginal cost and marginal benefit: the additional cost and additional benefit of one more decision. These ideas are the foundation of the cost-benefit analysis you’ll use throughout this course.
  • Opportunity cost: what you give up when you choose one option over the next-best alternative.

Chapter 1: The Decision Framework

Every organization succeeds or fails because of the decisions its leaders make. Chapter 1 introduces managerial economics as a practical framework for making better decisions. Instead of judging a decision by how it turned out, managerial economists first ask why it was made.

The Rational Actor Paradigm assumes people generally make the best decision they can based on the information they have and the incentives they face. That changes our job as consultants. Instead of assigning blame, we diagnose the decision. Did the decision-maker have enough information? Were the incentives aligned? Or was it both?

A few ideas to keep in mind before you begin:

  • Decision quality is not the same as outcome quality. Good decisions sometimes lead to bad outcomes, and bad decisions sometimes lead to good outcomes. A single result doesn’t tell you whether the original decision was sound. Your job is to evaluate the decision-making process, not simply how things turned out.
  • Why “no hindsight” matters. You already know how this story ends. Your client doesn’t. Instead of criticizing a past decision with information that wasn’t available at the time, your job is to recommend the best decision using only what AIG knew in January 2005.
  • What incentives really mean. Incentives have two parts: a way to measure performance and a reward or consequence tied to that performance. A decision-maker can have all the information in the world and still make a poor decision if incentives encourage the wrong behavior.

    Here’s a simple example that has nothing to do with AIG. Imagine a car salesperson pushes an extended warranty a customer probably doesn’t need. Is it an information problem because the salesperson genuinely believes it’s a good deal? Or is it an incentive problem because they earn a commission either way? The answer changes the solution. That’s exactly the kind of thinking Chapter 1 is asking you to practice.

Froeb argues that better decisions begin by asking the right questions before jumping to solutions. That mindset will guide every Board engagement this semester.

Client Situation

AIG has found itself in the middle of a housing-market opportunity. Through its Financial Products division, the company has become one of the market leaders in selling credit default swaps (CDS) that protect investors against losses on mortgage-backed securities. The business has grown rapidly and generated hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue while paying very few claims. From management’s perspective, this looks like exactly the kind of business you want to expand. Demand continues to grow, competitors are entering the market, and AIG has the reputation, expertise, and financial strength to capture an even larger share of the market.

But every good consultant knows there’s another side to every story, and that’s exactly why AIG’s Board wants an independent opinion.

Before approving another round of expansion, the Board hires 4400ECO Advisors to evaluate the decision. Your job is not to predict the future. Your job is to determine whether expanding AIG’s exposure to mortgage-backed securities through credit default swaps is a good decision based only on the information available in January 2005.

As consultants, you have one question to answer: Should AIG continue expanding its exposure to mortgage-backed securities through credit default swaps?

Economic Backdrop — Winter 2005

The Housing Boom

If you picked up a newspaper at the beginning of 2005, hardly anyone was predicting trouble. Home prices have been climbing nationally for roughly a decade, accelerating sharply after 2001. Homeownership is near record highs, home-equity extraction has become a common way for families to fund consumer spending and renovations, and mortgage origination volume is enormous. To most economists, homeowners, and lenders, this looks like a market working exactly as it should.

Interest Rates: The Fed’s “Measured Pace”

Since June 2004, the Federal Reserve, led by Alan Greenspan, has been gradually raising interest rates, increasing the federal funds rate by one-quarter of a percentage point at a time. Greenspan described this as a “measured pace.” Rates have climbed from the 46-year low of 1% reached earlier in the economic recovery, but by historical standards they remain relatively low. Cheap borrowing has helped fuel the housing boom, making mortgages more affordable and encouraging both homebuyers and investors to pour money into real estate. Most economists expect the Fed to continue raising rates gradually rather than making any sudden or dramatic moves.

The Rise of Mortgage Securitization

Banks weren’t just making mortgages anymore. They were bundling thousands of them together, selling them to investors around the world, and using that money to make even more loans. The system seemed to reward everyone involved. This lets banks originate more loans and move the associated risk off their balance sheets. Rating agencies have assigned strong ratings to large portions of these securities, based on historical default models built on decades of relatively stable, geographically diversified mortgage performance.

Early Skepticism

A small group of economists, regulators, and market analysts have started asking uncomfortable questions. Most people weren’t worried. That’s exactly why the Board hired you. Are home prices rising too quickly? Are lenders making loans to borrowers who may struggle to repay them? Has the financial system become so complicated that investors no longer understand the risks they’re taking? Most people dismiss these concerns. The market remains optimistic, profits continue to grow, and few believe a nationwide decline in housing prices is even possible.

What is a Credit Default Swap (CDS)? Think of a credit default swap as insurance on an investment instead of insurance on a house. Investors own mortgage-backed securities and pay AIG a regular premium for protection. If the mortgages continue performing and homeowners keep making their monthly payments, AIG simply collects the premiums. Everyone is happy. At least, that’s how it looks in January 2005.

If enough homeowners stop making their mortgage payments, however, the mortgage-backed securities lose value, and AIG may have to compensate investors for those losses.

In January 2005, this looks like an incredible business. AIG is collecting hundreds of millions of dollars in premiums, mortgage defaults remain relatively low, and the housing market appears stronger than ever. From the Board’s perspective, expanding the business seems like a logical next step.

Your job isn’t to predict the future. Your job is to decide whether this opportunity is as safe as it appears.

Where it stops looking like insurance:

InsuranceCredit Default Swap
Highly regulated. Insurance companies must hold reserves to help pay future claims.Largely unregulated in 2005. AIG was not required to hold the same level of reserves against potential CDS losses.
You need an insurable interest. You can’t buy insurance on your neighbor’s house.Not true for CDS. Investors could buy protection on debt they didn’t own, allowing them to speculate on whether borrowers would default.
Claims are paid after a loss occurs. A house has to burn down before insurance pays.Collateral could be required before a loss occurred. If the market value of the underlying securities fell, AIG could be required to post cash even if homeowners were still making their mortgage payments.
Most risks are independent. One house fire doesn’t make another house more likely.Mortgage risk was highly connected. If home prices fell across the country, many mortgage-backed securities could lose value at the same time.

This is where the comparison to homeowners insurance starts to fall apart. AIG wasn’t insuring one house. It was taking on the risk that an entire housing market could move together.

Digging deeper: why is this a diagnosis problem, not just a bad outcome problem?

It would be easy to look at this case and ask, “Was expanding the CDS business a good idea or a bad idea?” Chapter 1 asks a different and more useful question. Regardless of how the decision eventually turns out, was it a well-made decision based on what AIG knew in January 2005?

A good decision can still lead to a bad outcome if circumstances change or the world moves against you. A poor decision can also get lucky. Your job is not to grade the outcome. It is to diagnose the decision. Did AIG’s leadership have enough information? Did they have the right incentives? If not, which problem mattered most?

Here’s where the economics gets interesting and economists start asking different questions. At first glance, AIG’s CDS business looks like a home run. Mortgage default rates are low, premiums keep coming in, and the company has paid very few claims. Based on what most people know in January 2005, expanding the business seems like a smart decision. But a good consultant asks one more question.

What if the biggest risk isn’t homeowners defaulting?

AIG doesn’t necessarily have to wait for people to stop making their mortgage payments before it faces financial trouble. If investors lose confidence and the market value of mortgage-backed securities falls, some of AIG’s CDS contracts could require the company to post billions of dollars in additional collateral immediately, even if actual mortgage defaults remain low.

In other words, AIG could run into a cash problem long before it experiences large insurance losses.

That’s why your job is bigger than simply deciding whether the business is profitable. As consultants, you’re trying to determine whether AIG has reached the optimal level of risk. Every new CDS contract brings in additional revenue, but it also increases the company’s potential exposure. Your job is to decide whether the marginal benefit of writing one more contract is greater than its marginal cost, including the additional liquidity risk and collateral requirements that come with continued expansion.

A note on missing information.

You won’t have AIG Financial Products’ internal risk models or a precise breakdown of how its traders and executives are compensated — that level of detail isn’t public in January 2005. State a specific, reasonable assumption about what AIG’s decision-makers likely knew about collateral and liquidity risk, and how they were likely incentivized. Explain why your assumption is reasonable given what the case tells you, and carry it through your diagnosis consistently.

What we know

  • AIG’s revenue and claims history: AIG Financial Products has become a market leader in selling CDS protection on mortgage-backed securities, generating hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue while paying very few claims.
  • The housing market looks strong: mortgage default rates remain relatively low, home prices continue climbing, and rating agencies give many mortgage-backed securities their highest ratings.
  • Some CDS contracts carry collateral triggers: certain contracts can require AIG to post cash if the market value of the underlying securities falls, even before any homeowner actually defaults.
  • Competitive pressure is building: other firms are entering the credit-protection market, and AIG has the reputation, expertise, and financial strength to keep expanding its share.

What we don’t know (and must assume)

  • How AIG-FP is actually measuring risk: whether decision-makers are weighing collateral and liquidity risk as carefully as default risk, or leaning mainly on historical loss data that has stayed low so far.
  • How compensation is structured: what specifically triggers a bonus for AIG-FP’s traders and executives — premium volume written, current-period profit, or something tied to longer-run risk.
  • What oversight exists at the Board level: how closely AIG’s Board is tracking the growth of this specific business line versus AIG’s other, more traditional insurance operations.
The question before the Board: Looking only at the information available in January 2005, should AIG continue expanding its credit default swap business?

As you make your recommendation, separate what AIG’s decision-makers actually knew from what they were incentivized to do and decide whether the real problem is an information gap, an incentive gap, or both.
