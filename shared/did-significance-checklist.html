Is This DiD Result Actually Important?

A self-check for difference-in-differences results, grounded in Ziliak & McCloskey's case against mistaking statistical significance for economic significance

Before anything else, ask Am I focused on the size of the effect, or am I letting statistical significance carry the argument instead?
1. Did I test the actual difference-in-differences quantity, or just two separate before/after comparisons?
Your claim is about one number: (change in treatment group) − (change in control group). Your regression output should give you that difference directly, usually as the coefficient on an interaction term (Treatment × Post), with its own standard error and test statistic. Don't let two separate "before vs. after" t-tests, one per group, stand in for it.
Sounds like the mistake"The treatment group's outcome changed significantly (p<0.05); the control group's didn't change significantly — so the treatment worked." Two separate tests doing the job one direct test should do.
2. Have I reported my effect size in units a reader can picture?
For example, translate your DiD coefficient into a dollar figure or a change in jobs per firm, whatever unit fits your outcome.
What good looks likeAngrist's schooling paper reports wages "37 percent higher," a number a reader can picture.
3. Have I said whether that size is big or small — compared to what?
Compare your number to something real: a benchmark from the literature, or a cost a policymaker would actually weigh. Without that comparison, a number doesn't tell your reader — or you — whether it matters.
Sounds like the mistakeReporting "the effect is 2.1 units" and stopping there, with no sentence on whether 2.1 is trivial or enormous in this context.
4. Am I doing "asterisk econometrics" — treating the most-starred coefficient as the most important one?
Are you emphasizing a result because it cleared p<0.05, or because it's large? Those are different criteria, and they can point to different coefficients.
5. Am I doing "sign econometrics" — reporting the sign and stopping?
Look for your own version of "the coefficient is negative, as expected" with no follow-up on magnitude.
Red flag in the wildBernheim & Wantz (1995), quoted in Ziliak & McCloskey: "the coefficients are all negative... [but] not statistically significant." Sign given, size never discussed.
6. Have I reported a confidence interval, not just a point estimate and a p-value?
A confidence interval shows the range of plausible effect sizes, which is what you need to argue economic importance. A bare p-value only tells you the distance from zero.
7. Have I checked whether my treatment and control groups are similar in size — and if not, discussed power?
A "not significant" result from a small or lopsided sample may simply mean your test lacked the power to detect a real effect. Say so if it applies.
Sounds like the mistakeTreating "not significant" as proof of "no effect" without ever mentioning sample sizes or power.
8. (Bonus) Does my result hold up on anything beyond the exact sample I estimated it on?
Check whether your result survives outside the data that produced it, for example with a placebo test or a check of pre-trends. Ziliak and McCloskey's own correspondence calls this kind of testing a good argument, just not a necessary one. Consider it a bonus.
Grounded in Ziliak, S. T. and McCloskey, D. N., "Size Matters: The Standard Error of Regressions in the American Economic Review," Econ Journal Watch 1(2), August 2004, pp. 331-358; and The Cult of Statistical Significance (University of Michigan Press, 2008).
