Section 2 What Is This Course About?

Elon University Catalog description for MBA 6250: Essential Economics for Strategic Management (3 credits)

Future managers, entrepreneurs, and business strategists practice applying the economic way of thinking. You will begin by reviewing the basics of cost-benefit analysis and market competition. Using this intuition, you will consider more advanced topics. For example, you will examine the importance of product differentiation and learn how to use pricing strategies such as group pricing and targeted discounts. You will also judge how your views of markets might change when market power, neighborhood effects, and information problems are present. Lastly, you will consider how to think strategically when your best choices depend on what others choose.

Section 3 Why Should You Take This Course?

Managers spend their careers trying to get other people (employees, divisions, trading partners) to act in the firm's best interest, usually without full information about what those people know or are actually doing. Essential Economics for Strategic Management provides the analytical toolkit for diagnosing and solving that problem.

This course applies microeconomic theory to the problems that practitioners actually encounter. The central method is problem solving: each module is introduced through a managerial question, analyzed with a precise framework, and applied to real cases. The Froeb et al. textbook is deliberately lean and decision-focused: every chapter is organized around a type of decision, not an abstract theoretical concept.

The course also gives you practice at a different scale: in Module 4, you train on IMPLAN Cloud, then apply it during the final exam to run a regional economic impact analysis. It applies the same economic way of thinking to a whole region instead of one choice. Employers value the ability to reason through a single decision, but they value just as much the ability to model what a new manufacturing plant does to a region's jobs and income.

By the end of the course, you should be able to identify the economic structure of a business problem, apply the appropriate analytical framework, and communicate your reasoning clearly, in writing, in a case analysis, and in a professional capstone deliverable.

Section 4 What Will You Be Able to Do?
  • 1 Diagnose organizational problems using the incentive-alignment framework: identify who is making a bad decision, whether they have enough information to decide well, and whether they have the incentive to do so.
  • 2 Apply cost-benefit reasoning to managerial decisions, correctly identifying the relevant costs of a decision while avoiding the sunk-cost and hidden-cost fallacies.
  • 3 Use game-theoretic reasoning (including Nash equilibrium) to anticipate competitor behavior in sequential- and simultaneous-move games.
  • 4 Identify adverse-selection and moral-hazard problems arising from information asymmetry, and evaluate screening, signaling, and monitoring solutions.
  • 5 Design incentive contracts and organizational structures (for employees, divisions, and vertical trading partners) that align agents' incentives with the firm's goals.
  • 6 Communicate an economic analysis clearly and concisely in a professional deliverable aimed at a non-economist decision-maker.

Section 7 How Will Your Learning Be Assessed?
Case Analyses (×4)

One case analysis for each of four selected modules (Modules 3, 5, 7, 9). Each case presents a real or realistic managerial decision scenario. You will identify the economic problem, apply the module's framework, and recommend a course of action backed by supporting analysis. Submit each case via Canvas, 400–600 words plus any supporting quantitative work. AI disclosure required if used.

Final Exam

An in-person, closed-book synthesis exam during the university final exam period, December 7 (7:00–9:00 p.m.). The prompts are case-style, drawn from across the whole course. You diagnose the problem type and apply the right framework without being told which module it came from. One prompt is the Module 4 regional economic impact analysis in IMPLAN Cloud, the macro complement to this course's focus on individual decisions. Module 4 itself is practice-only. Nothing is submitted or graded that week. Module 10 gives you a full week to prepare; nothing is due until the exam period itself.

Problem Set

One problem set for the whole semester: eight problems across five chapters (Ch 3, 15, 19, 20, 22), selected for their calculation focus. Show your work. I grade it by hand. One due date for the whole set: Wednesday, December 2. But work through each chapter's problems the same week as that module's quiz and Perusall discussion, so the work stays spread across the term instead of piling up at the deadline.

Perusall Discussions (×8)

In Perusall, you annotate a short scenario every content module, using that module's framework. Reply to at least one classmate for real, not just to agree. The full rubric is in the Perusall Discussion Guide, linked from each module.

Formative Quizzes (×7)

Seven content modules get a multiple-choice self-check drawn from a shared item bank. Module 1 has no chapter quiz. It runs on Perusall alone. You get three attempts, and there's no partial credit: score at least 80% on your best one for full credit. You're allowed to be wrong on the way to getting it right, as long as you get there by your third try.

Section 8 How Can I Use AI in This Class?

AI Conditional Use Policy Statement

When used appropriately, you may employ AI tools to support your learning in this course. For example, using AI tools to quiz you over course material, prompting AI tools to explain or clarify course concepts, or using AI tools as instructed by your professor for a class activity or assignment are all allowable uses. However, using AI tools in ways that bypass learning is not considered appropriate. Submitting AI generated work as your own, using AI tools to answer questions during exams or assessments when prohibited by your professor, or other similar uses of AI are inappropriate and represent Elon Honor Code violations. If you are uncertain if your use of AI tools is appropriate, ask your professor.
