Discovery — Finding Your Story

Your reference sheet has the six elements and three attributes — this page is just today's work.

This is Discovery, the first step in Mind & Voice — the personal narrative due later this semester. Nothing here is graded. The work is finding a real decision from your own life and getting far enough into it that The Scene, when you write it in Eli Review on October 9, is a draft you're ready to start.

Before you start: who are you writing this for?

Picture a friend from home, someone who's never taken an economics course. Hold them in mind through every question below. What do they need to know to follow your story, and what can you skip because they'd already get it?

1. Agent — who decided?

Name the person who made the choice: you, or someone you watched closely enough to understand why they did it. Your reader needs enough about them to believe this decision was theirs to make.

2. Action — what did they choose?

State the choice plainly, as fact, and save the motive for question 6. Then name the road not taken: without a real alternative, what you have is just an event.

3. Time — when did it happen?

Pin it to something specific — a particular day, a particular conversation. Note how long the deciding itself took, an instant or weeks of going back and forth.

4. Place — where did it happen?

The physical place: what you could see, hear, and smell there, concrete enough that your reader could stand in it.

5. Manner — how did it unfold?

Alone or with someone weighing in, fast or slow, under pressure or with room to think? What did the moment of deciding actually look like from outside?

6. Cause — why this, and not the other thing?

This is the hardest question, and it matters most. Weigh what they gained by choosing this against the cost of walking away from the alternative, and note what they knew, or didn't know, at the time. Answer honestly, in your own words — don't reach for economics vocabulary yet. That answer is where the reasoning of the story lives, even though the finished piece won't name it as economics.

Manner vs. Cause, same distinction as on your reference sheet: Manner is how someone acted: the observable style, like "calmly" or "quickly." Cause is why they acted: the motive underneath, usually unstated, like "because they were scared of losing the apartment."

Answer what you can before class — a gap here isn't a problem. That's what Discovery is for.
