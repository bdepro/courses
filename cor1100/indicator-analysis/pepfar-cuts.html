Section II  ~20 min
Connecting to the Introduction and Ch. II — The Great Division, Caring for Others
Hanley opens the book naming a divide between self-concern and other-concern. This episode plays that divide out at national scale.
Hanley Concept What it predicts (fill in) Apply it to the episode
The great division (Intro) Self-concern and other-concern pull against each other especially hard when the "other" is and unfamiliar. Does distance make the sympathy Hanley describes in Ch. II weaker, or should it still apply undiminished? Defend your answer.
Natural interest in others (Ch. II) Humans have an original, unprompted concern for others' happiness that doesn't require to exist. Does a national budget decision get to override an individual instinct like this — and should it?
Self-interest at scale A government's budget priorities can stand in for an individual's at a much larger scale. Does scaling self-interest up to a national level change its moral weight, according to Hanley's framework — or is it the same problem, just bigger?
Stress-Test the Claim

A budget director says foreign aid is "a hard tradeoff, not a moral failure." Using Hanley's account of natural sympathy for others in Ch. II, give one reason this framing understates what's at stake, and one reason it might still be a defensible position for someone managing competing obligations.

Synthesis  ~15 min
Write: Does Distance Excuse Indifference?

Someone tells you they don't feel responsible for a program cut that affects people overseas because "they're not my problem." Using Ch. II's claim that sympathy for others is natural, not a bonus, and the Introduction's "great division," explain what's wrong (or right) about that response — and where you personally draw the line on how far your own sympathy is supposed to reach.
