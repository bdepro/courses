Why Why Are You Doing Community Engagement in COR 1100: The Global Experience?

Hanley opens this book with a claim worth testing outside the classroom: concern for others is just as natural as self-interest. Ch. II calls this sympathy, an unprompted interest in a stranger’s happiness you don’t have to be talked into. I want you to test that claim on people you don’t already know, in a county most of you didn’t grow up in.

Alamance County is part of this course. Elon sits inside it, and the partnership between the two runs back more than a century. This assignment puts you inside that partnership — with its people, and with the land, water, and natural resources that shape their daily lives. You’ll take one side of the great division between self-concern and other-concern, then write honestly about what you found.

The Kernodle Center for Civic Life handles the logistics. You’ll pick a service opportunity, sign up through their page, and answer a set of reflection questions before and after. What you bring to it is Hanley’s vocabulary (sympathy, the great division, the impartial spectator) and the willingness to check your assumptions against what actually happens once you show up.

Phase 1 Pre-Experience

Ch. II argues that caring about a stranger’s welfare doesn’t require knowing them first. The questions below ask you to name the assumptions you’re already carrying about Alamance County and the people you’ll work alongside, in writing, before you can talk yourself out of admitting what they are.

Read, then discuss or jot a few lines on your own:

  • Alamance County at a Glance — How does this compare to the community you’re coming from? To what you assumed about Alamance County? What was the most interesting piece of information, and why?
  • Achieving Excellence Together: A Century of Community Partnership with Alamance County — What did you learn about Elon’s relationship with Alamance County? How can students get involved locally? Why does a university partnering with its surrounding community matter?

Then sign up for your service opportunity:

Select one opportunity through the Kernodle Center for Civic Life’s Phoenix Serve page on GivePulse. Do this early — some opportunities fill.

Answer these before you go (part of your written submission, see Submit below):

  1. What do you know about your potential community partner, and what do you hope to learn about them or the people they serve?
  2. What assumptions are you making about your community partner and the people they serve?
  3. What assumptions might they make about you?
  4. What are your goals for this experience? What skills or experiences do you hope to gain?

Phase 2 Experience

Show up, do the work you signed up for, and pay attention to what surprises you. Post-Experience asks you to use exactly that material.

Phase 3 Post-Experience

An impartial spectator, per Ch. XVII, judges a situation without the stake you had in it. You don’t get that distance from your own service, but you can get close by holding what you expected against how the day actually went. That comparison is what the seven questions below are actually for.

  1. What did you learn — about yourself or your field of study — during your service experience?
  2. What surprised you about your service experience?
  3. How did your experience compare to the assumptions you held about your community partner beforehand?
  4. What did you notice about the physical place where you served — the land, water, green space, or built environment — and how might it shape the daily lives of the people your community partner serves?
  5. In what ways were you successful at meeting your commitments to your community partner, and what contributed to that success?
  6. In what ways were you challenged by your service experience?
  7. What else would you like to learn to advance in your field or related areas? What will you do to build on this experience?

Once you’ve answered these, take a look at the Civic Engagement Pathways Hub to see how you might stay involved beyond this one assignment.
